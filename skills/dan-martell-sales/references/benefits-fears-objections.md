# Benefits, fears and objections

**How to read this file.** Martell has not published a list of client objections to AI agents. His AI videos speak to owners building their own agents. His sales videos cover objections in general. So each item is marked:

- **[Martell]** he says this directly
- **[Applied]** his method, applied to MIX2.AI by us
- **[MIX2]** comes from MIX2.AI's own facts and website, not from him

## What an AI agent is, in plain words

- **[Martell]** A chat is like a meeting. An agent is like an employee. You ask a chat a question and get an answer. You give an agent a job and it runs the whole workflow.
- **[Martell]** Three levels: chat answers, workflows follow a process, agents work towards outcomes using context.
- **[Martell]** An agent diagnoses, assembles a plan, takes action and assesses its own work, in a loop. Without the loop it is just an automation.

## The benefits he identifies

Use only the two or three that match what the prospect told you. Martell is clear that extra benefits confuse buyers.

| Benefit **[Martell]** | Plain version | His example |

## The fears

Say these out loud before the prospect has to. Treat the fear as reasonable. Martell's own line on letting go is that these are real fears.

### "If I let it act on its own, it will do something wrong"
- **His view [Martell]:** Building is the easy part. Letting it act without you is the scary part. You don't hand over the keys on day one.
- **His four stages [Martell]:** set guardrails first, approve everything at first, loosen the leash as trust grows, then give it a schedule to run on.
- **Say:** "You wouldn't give a new hire the company card on day one, and this is the same. We start with it drafting and you approving. You decide what it's allowed to touch." **[Applied]**
- **Backed by [MIX2]:** teams can start read-only, and you set the rules during the day.

### "It will replace people, or my team will think so"
- **His view [Martell]:** When he showed an agent to his executive assistant, she thought she was out of a job. It freed her for higher-value work, and he'd rather pay her for that. He has also said people ask what AI means for their job, business and family, and that he's optimistic: time back lets people be more human.
- **Be careful:** he also says one of his companies is close to replacing its whole sales team with AI. Don't pretend AI never removes work. Be honest that the task mix changes.
- **Say:** "That's the right question to ask, and your team will ask it too. What we work on is the repetitive part of a role, so people spend more time on the work you hired them for. How you handle that with your team is a leadership decision, and it's better made in the open." **[Applied]**

### "I'm already behind, and I don't understand this"
- **His view [Martell]:** He says he has accepted he will always feel behind. He also says it's easier than it looks, and that if you can explain a task, the AI can do it.
- **Say:** "Nobody's on top of all of it, and the people who sound most confident usually aren't either. You don't need to catch up on everything. You need one real thing that works for you." **[Applied]**
- **Backed by [MIX2]:** no technical background or perfect idea needed.

### "It will make mistakes and embarrass me"
- **His view [Martell]:** One of his agents got a friend's wife's name wrong. His fix was better context, plus an instruction never to guess and to ask if unsure. He keeps agents in review mode at first, and they learn from what he changes.
- **Don't repeat** his claim elsewhere that AI doesn't make mistakes. His own story shows it does.
- **Say:** "It will make mistakes, especially early on. That's why it starts in draft mode with you checking it, so mistakes are cheap and nobody outside sees them." **[Applied]**

### "I'll lose control, or what makes us special"
- **His view [Martell]:** From his book: people fear losing their fingerprint on the work, being made to look bad, or even losing the business. He uses the 10-80-10 rule: you shape the first 10%, others do the middle 80%, and you refine the last 10%.
- **Also [Martell]:** Build agents around outcomes and a definition of done, not every step. Otherwise it's like hiring a driver and keeping your hand on the wheel.
- **Say:** "You stay at the start and the end. You say what good looks like and you check the result. It handles the middle." **[Applied]**

### "It will touch our money, customers or private data"
- **His view [Martell]:** His finance agent is read-only and told never to move money. His sales agent is told never to message a customer. Each agent stays in a narrow lane.
- **Say:** "Those limits are set by you. Read-only and draft-only are normal starting points, and sensitive information can stay out entirely." **[Applied]**

### "My team won't keep up, or won't use it"
- **His view [Martell]:** When you speed up, your team has to keep up. If one person's output jumps, everyone around them has to handle more. He got his leaders using the tools together and taught the whole team.
- **Say:** "That's why it's worth doing this with one trusted colleague or a team, rather than one person racing ahead." **[Applied]**

### "It's too big, I don't know where to start"
- **His view [Martell]:** Don't give it everything. Narrow the scope, one agent for one job. If you can't state the outcome in one sentence, you aren't ready to build.
- **Say:** "We'd start with one task you repeat every week. Just one." **[Applied]**

## The objections

For all of them, Martell's pattern is the same: acknowledge, dig deeper, then respond. Then go back to the ask.

| They say | Source | What's underneath | What to say | Prevent it next time |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| "That's expensive" | [Martell] | The value wasn't connected to their pain, usually because it was never quantified. | "That makes sense. Can I go back to something? You said this takes about [X] hours a week. What does that cost you over a year?" | Quantify in Step 2. |
| "Not right now" | [Martell] | Wrong customer, wrong priority or a hidden concern. | "I appreciate you telling me. Often when people say that, something else is unresolved and it's easier to put it off. Is that true here?" | Ask if it's a now thing. |
| "Send me a proposal" | [Martell] | Politely avoiding a decision. Information doesn't make decisions. | "Happy to. What would you most like to see in it? And let's book 15 minutes to go through it together. Does Thursday or Friday suit you?" | Clear decision-makers early. |
| "I need to check with my partner, board or IT" | [Martell] | A decision-maker wasn't identified. | "Of course. What will they want to know? Would it help if we had that conversation together?" | Ask who else is involved. |
| "We have no budget" | [Martell] | Real, or low priority. | "You mentioned a budget of [X] earlier. What changed?" If there was none: "Is that for this year, or at all?" | Ask about budget before the offer. |
| "We don't have time" | [Martell] | Priorities have not been dropped. | "You said you could block a day. What would you need to stop doing to make that possible?" | Ask about time before the offer. |
| "It doesn't connect to [our system]" | [Martell] | A specific process need. | "Thanks for saying so. Can we dig into that? How would you want the information to flow, and who's involved?" Then look for a workaround. **[MIX2]** Connections aren't part of a Build Day, so offer a scoped quote. | Ask about their tools early. |
| "We can do this ourselves" | [Martell] | Overconfidence. | "You might well. What's stopped it happening so far?" | Ask what they've tried. |
| "Let me think about it" | [Applied] | Something is unclear or unsaid. | "Of course. Usually that means something isn't quite right yet. What's the part you're least sure about?" | Check for agreement in the recap. |
| "We tried AI and it didn't stick" | [Applied] | Too broad, no clear outcome, poor context. | "What did you try, and what happened?" Then show what's different: one task and a written definition of done. | Ask what they've tried. |
| "We already have Copilot or ChatGPT" | [Applied] | They see tools as the same as results. | "Good, that's a head start. How is it being used day to day? Are people chatting with it, or has it taken over a piece of work?" | Ask about current tools. |
| "We're hiring someone for this" | [Applied] | Specific to the lead list. A hire is a sign of need, not a no. | "That's a good sign. What will they own? It often helps when leaders know first hand what they're asking that person to do." | Ask what's behind the hire. |
| "Who else have you done this for?" | [MIX2] | Reasonable concern about risk. | "We're new, so I won't give you client names. That's why we put our fee on the line. If you leave without the working first version we agreed in writing, you get it back." | Don't hide it. |
| "What if it doesn't work?" | [MIX2] | Risk. | Explain the guarantee and its limits from the website FAQ. It covers a working first version, not business results. | Mention the guarantee with the price. |
| "Is our data safe? What about GDPR?" | [MIX2] | A real duty. | "It's the right question. You decide on the day what can be connected and what stays out, and we can start read-only. I can't give you a legal view, so involve whoever owns that for you. A security review is separate work." | Ask who owns data decisions. |
| "Will you keep it running afterwards?" | [MIX2] | A signal of future need. | "You'll have what you built and a list of next steps. Running it for you isn't something we offer today." Record the answer. | Be clear about what the day delivers. |
| "We're not technical" | [MIX2] | Fear of looking foolish. | "You don't need to be. If you can use email and a browser, you can take part, and you won't write code." | Build them up in Step 2. |

## Reading the buyer

Martell's five Time Assassins describe how people lose time. We use them as a guide to how a buyer behaves. **[Applied]** Never say these labels to a prospect.

| Type | On a call | What helps |
|---|---|---|
| Staller | Can't decide | Remind them no decision is still a decision, and agree a small next step with a date. |
| Speed Demon | Wants to rush | Slow down and complete the diagnosis so they buy the right thing. |
| Supervisor | Wants to control every step | Use trust in stages and the 10-80-10 rule. |
| Saver | Focuses on cost | Use their own numbers. Martell notes that saving every euro also stops growth. |
