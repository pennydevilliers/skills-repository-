# The 45-minute call playbook

Based on Martell's connect, diagnose, prescribe, commit process, fitted to MIX2.AI's free 45-minute conversation. The timings are a suggestion, not his. These are talk tracks: know the point of each line and say it in your own words.

## Before the call (10 minutes)

- Read the job ad, the company site and the contact's public profile.
- Check the contact's role is current. Lead list titles can be wrong.
- Write down your guess at their problem, then be ready to drop it.
- Have the prices, the guarantee wording and two possible dates ready.
- Check they are qualified: a leader or team with a real problem, some awareness of that problem, and the means to pay.

**Avoid the double sale.** If someone doesn't know they have a problem, you must sell them the problem and then the solution. Companies hiring AI coaches, adoption leads or implementers already know, which is why they are good early adopters.

## Step 1: Connect (0 to 5 minutes)

Don't open by talking about MIX2.AI. Trust comes from respecting their time, asking good questions and understanding their situation.

**Set the frame:**
> "Thanks for making the time. We have 45 minutes. I'd like to ask about your business and where AI might matter. If I think we can help, I'll tell you what that looks like and what it costs. If I don't, I'll say so, and you'll still leave with a clearer view of where to start. Does that work for you?"

**If you plan to record:**
> "Would you mind if I record this for my notes? It stays with us, and it's fine if you'd rather not."

**Why us:**
> "Before we start, what made you say yes to this conversation?"

**Why now:**
> "What's true in your business right now that makes AI a priority?"

**If you contacted them because of a job ad:**
> "I saw you're hiring an AI coach. What's behind that decision?"

## Step 2: Diagnose (5 to 25 minutes)

This is the longest step. Your questions are how they judge whether you're an expert, so keep them specific. Write down their answers word for word, because you'll use them in Step 3.

**Where they want to be.** Start here, because people share goals more easily than problems.
> "Imagine it's 12 months from now and we're celebrating, because AI has really worked for you. What happened?"

**Where they are today.** Use their own goal as the bridge.
> "You said you want your managers spending less time on reporting. Where is that today?"

**What's in the way:**
> "What's stopping you getting there?"
> "What else?"

**What they've tried.** Martell treats this as essential. If you skip it, you'll be fighting a concern you can't see.
> "What have you tried so far? What happened?"

**The problem behind the problem.** What people name first is often not the real issue.
> "When that happens, what does it hold up?"
> "Why do you think that keeps happening?"

**Find the right work for AI.** Use his three tests:

| Test | Question |
|---|---|
| Repetitive | "How often does this happen?" |
| Rules-based | "Does it follow roughly the same steps each time?" |
| Return on time | "How long does it take, and who does it?" |

If a task happens rarely, has no clear process or wouldn't repay the effort of building something, say so. Plain chat may be enough.

**Quantify it.** Martell says a price objection usually means this step was skipped.
> "Roughly how many hours a week go into that? Who does it? What would you rather they did with that time?"

Use their numbers and show the sum as their estimate. For example, 4 hours a week at a cost of €90 an hour over 46 working weeks comes to €16,560 a year. The 46 weeks is an assumption, so adjust it. This is not a promise of savings.

**Use his Buyback Rate if it helps a leader value their own time.** Annual income divided by 2,000 hours gives an hourly value. Divide that by 4 to get the most you'd pay someone else to take a task over. On €200,000 that's €100 an hour, and €25 an hour to buy it back. He uses it to show that a leader doing €10 tasks is holding the business back. Ask for a rough figure only if they're comfortable sharing one.

**Find the energy, not just the cost.** This comes from the Time and Energy Audit in *Buy Back Your Time*.
> "Which part of your week drains you most, and could be done by someone else?"
> "If you got those hours back, what would you do with them?"

The last question matters most. Martell's point is that time back is only worth something when it goes to higher-value work.

**Bring them to the buying zone.** Notice which way they lean:

| They sound | What to do | Say |
|---|---|---|
| Overconfident ("we can do this ourselves") | Bring them down gently | "It sounds like you have a plan. What's stopped it happening so far?" |
| Uncertain ("we're not ready") | Build them up | "That's a sensible way to feel. You don't need to be technical, and you don't need the perfect idea." |

**Don't consult for free.** Martell warns that if you hand over every answer on the call, you release the pressure and they have no reason to buy. Show that you understand the problem, and keep the build for the day.

## Step 2b: Clear the obstacles (25 to 30 minutes)

Ask these before you offer anything. Later, these answers are what you refer back to.

**Who decides:**
> "Besides you, who else would need to be comfortable with this?"
> "Does your IT team need to be involved?"

**Budget:**
> "Have you set aside a budget for this?"
> "If the right option were on the table, what would you be comfortable investing?"

**Time:**
> "If this were the right thing, could you block a full day in the next six weeks?"

**Priority:**
> "Is this something you want to solve now, or later this year?"

If it's clearly "later", find out why before you go on.

## Step 3: Prescribe (30 to 38 minutes)

**Recap and get agreement:**
> "Let me check I've got this. You want to get to [their goal]. Today you're at [their situation]. What's in the way is [blocker one], [blocker two] and [blocker three]. Did I get that right?"

**Hand them the choice:**
> "I've been asking a lot of questions. Where would you like to go from here?"

Most people will ask what working together looks like, so now they've invited the pitch. If they don't, ask whether they'd like to hear how you'd approach it.

**Offer one thing.** A confused mind doesn't buy, so don't list every option.

| What you heard | Offer |
|---|---|
| A leader or small leadership group working out what AI means for them | Leadership Build Day |
| A team with a real business challenge | Team Build Day |
| They want a written plan and a check-in | Build Day + Blueprint |
| Up to 15 people, with support over weeks | 6-Week Sprint |
| Something built and connected to their systems | AI Development & Integration, scoped on request |
| Someone to run it for them on an ongoing basis | Not available yet. Say so and record it. |

**Map it to their blockers only.** Leave out everything else, however good it is.
> "You said nobody has time to learn this. On the day you'd have Jacques and me beside you, so you're building rather than studying."
> "You said the last pilot went nowhere because it was too broad. We'd pick one weekly task and agree in writing what working means."

Then go to the price and the ask in `closing.md`.

## If it isn't a fit

Say it plainly, suggest what might help instead, and ask whether anyone else comes to mind. This builds the trust the whole approach depends on.

## After every call (5 minutes)

Martell says this is where the gold is, and that most people skip it. Log:

- The names, roles and who else decides
- Their goal, their situation and their blockers, in their words
- What they've tried
- Their numbers
- Fears and objections raised
- The exact next step and date
- For MIX2: their answer to "What would you want running in your business that you cannot build alone?"

Share the notes with Jacques so nobody has to ask the client the same thing twice.
