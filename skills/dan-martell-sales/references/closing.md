# How to close

Martell prefers the word "enrolling" to "closing", because you are starting something rather than ending a conversation. If you've followed the process, the close feels natural. A close that feels forced usually means an earlier step was skipped.

## The sequence (38 to 45 minutes)

### 1. Say the price plainly, then keep going

Don't stop and wait after the number. Martell gives the price, moves straight to one more benefit tied to a worry they raised, then asks. For MIX2 that benefit is naturally the guarantee.

> "The Leadership Build Day is €1,850 excl. VAT for up to two people. And because you said you've been burned by a pilot that went nowhere, we agree in writing what a working first version has to do, and if you leave without it, you get your fee back."

Check current prices and guarantee terms on the website before each call.

### 2. Ask, assuming they'll go ahead

Don't ask "Are you ready to move forward?" Offer a choice between two ways of going ahead. His phrase for this is a "this or that".

> "Would the 8th or the 10th work better?"
> "Would you bring one colleague, or come on your own?"
> "Are you working in Microsoft 365, or shall we use Claude?"

### 3. Stop talking

Martell's version of the old rule: the first person to speak after the offer loses the sale. Let the silence do its work.

### 4. Handle what comes up

> "That makes total sense."

Then deal with it using `benefits-fears-objections.md`, and ask again. Refer back to what they told you in Step 2b about budget, time and decision-makers.

### 5. Make it real on the call

Martell's rule is to collect payment on the call, using "What card do you want to put that on?" He says never to confuse a promise with a payment.

**Adapted for MIX2:** this is Dutch B2B, and MIX2 invoices with payment due before the day. A commitment on the call means all of these:

- A date in the diary
- Who receives the invoice, and whether a purchase order or supplier registration is needed
- Who attends, and the tool they'll use
- A time to agree the written definition of a working first version

> "Great, I'll hold the 8th for you. Who should the invoice go to, and do you need a purchase order number on it?"

### 6. Lock it in

He calls this the takeaway close. It surfaces doubts while you can still deal with them.

> "I'm looking forward to this. It'll get at [their three problems]. Can I ask one thing? Apart from a global financial meltdown, is there any reason this wouldn't go ahead?"

If they name one, deal with it now.

## If they can't decide on the call

### Book a meeting from a meeting

Martell calls this BAMFAM. Never end with "I'll send it over" and nothing booked.

1. Ask what they need in order to decide.
2. Book the next call while you're both there. Send the invite and ask them to accept it.
3. Ask for the favour:

> "Could you do me a favour? If anything comes up, could you let me know beforehand, so Jacques and I can plan around it?"

Martell's version says you'll give the slot to someone else. Only say that if it's true.

### Never just send a proposal

> "I'll put that together. What would you most want to see in it? Let's take 15 minutes on Thursday to walk through it, so it doesn't sit in your inbox."

## Follow up

He says the fortunes are made in the follow-up, and that most people don't do it.

- **Never write "just following up".** He says if you use those words, you've lost the sale.
- **Bring something useful,** connected to what they told you, in a relaxed tone.

> "Hi [name], I came across this article on [their exact issue] and thought of what you said about [their words]. I hope it's useful. If you'd like to pick up the conversation about the team's reporting, I'd be glad to."

- **Check the pipeline often:** deals stuck too long in a stage, leads with no follow-up, and calls with no next step. This is what his sales agent does every night.
- **Don't invent reasons to get in touch.** A real article, a real idea or a real change is fine.
- **Every outreach email needs an easy opt-out,** as the lead spec requires.

## If you lose it

- Ask why. Amateurs move on and learn nothing.
- Review the call with the four questions in `SKILL.md`.
- Martell's view of a "no" that came late: if you reached the end and got no commitment, a step was rushed or skipped.

## After the sale

He calls this servicing the sale, and says the post-sale process is worth more than the transaction.

**Overselling is the real risk.** He warns that overpromising leads to cancellations and unhappy buyers. A sale someone regrets isn't a sale.

**Hand over cleanly,** so the client never has to repeat themselves.

**Ask for introductions at the "wow" moment.** He says the moment to ask is the first time the client is genuinely delighted, which for MIX2 may be the end of the Build Day or the one-month call.

> "Is there one other leader like you who should see this?"
> "Would you mind introducing us, or may I mention your name?"

No referral fees are on offer, so this is a simple favour.

**Follow the pace in the strategy note:** send what was built within a week, then call at about one month. Ask what's still running and what it saved, and ask for a testimonial and written permission to use the story. Those are the first real proof points MIX2 will have.

## Early adopter offer

This is an option for Penny to decide on, not a live offer. **Don't offer it until she approves.** In his pre-sell video, Martell recommends a limited early adopter group with a discount in exchange for payment upfront and feedback. He also says you know someone is engaged when they start giving you advice, and that ten conversations is the minimum before you judge an idea. It could suit MIX2's need for its first three clients, but any discount and its wording are a pricing decision.
