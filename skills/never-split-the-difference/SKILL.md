# Never Split the Difference (Chris Voss approach)

This skill applies the negotiation method from Chris Voss's book *Never Split the Difference*, co-written with Tahl Raz, and his Black Swan Group teaching. It is a paraphrased working method, not his text, so say "using Chris Voss's approach" rather than speaking as him or quoting at length.

For deeper detail on techniques, negotiator types, the full Ackerman worked example, and sources, read `references/concepts.md`. If `references/transcripts/` has files, such as talks, MasterClass notes, or the user's book notes, prefer their framing.

## The core idea

**Negotiation is emotional before it's rational.** People decide with feelings and justify with logic. So the job is to discover what's really driving the other side, make them feel understood, and let them talk themselves into a solution, rather than arguing your case.

- **Don't split the difference.** Meeting halfway is a lazy compromise that often leaves both sides with a bad outcome, like wearing one black shoe and one brown. Aim for the real deal, which usually comes from information you don't have yet.
- **Tactical empathy** means understanding the other side's feelings and worldview, and saying them out loud. It is not agreeing or being soft. You can be empathetic and still hold firm.
- **Listening is the weapon.** The goal is to uncover "Black Swans": hidden, unknown pieces of information that change everything. Most come from listening, not talking.
- **"No" starts the negotiation.** "Yes" is often fake, said to make you go away. "No" makes people feel safe and in control, and "that's right" is the breakthrough you want.

## Ethics

These are influence techniques. Use them to understand people and find better deals, not to deceive. Don't invent fake deadlines, fake competing offers, or false facts in scripts you write. If the user asks for that, suggest an honest version of the same move. Mirroring and labeling work best when they are genuine.

## How to help

First work out which mode the user is in, then follow that mode's steps.

### Mode 1: Prepare a negotiation (build a one-sheet)

Ask briefly for what you don't know: what they want, who the counterpart is, the relationship, deadlines, and the alternatives on both sides. Then produce a one-sheet with these parts:

1. **Goal:** an ambitious but justified target. Think best case, not bottom line, because a bottom line pulls people toward it. State it in writing.
2. **Summary:** two to three sentences of known facts that the other side would agree with ("that's right").
3. **Labels and accusation audit:** three to five labels for their likely feelings, and the worst things they might think about the user or the deal, phrased to say up front.
4. **Calibrated questions:** three to five "what" and "how" questions to uncover value and obstacles, including deal-killers that sit behind the table, such as a boss, a committee, or budget owners.
5. **Non-cash offers:** things that are cheap for the user and valuable to the other side, to trade instead of conceding on price.
6. **Ackerman plan,** if there's a number involved. Use the steps in "Bargaining" below.

Also predict the counterpart's **type**, which shapes tone:
- **Analyst:** methodical; hates surprises; silence means they're thinking. Bring data and give them time.
- **Accommodator:** relationship-first and friendly, but may overpromise. Pin down how they'll actually deliver.
- **Assertive:** wants to be heard and wants speed. Let them speak first, and use labels and summaries.

### Mode 2: "What do I say?" (live help)

Give exact words the user can say or send, and give two or three options at most. Prefer this order:

1. **Label or mirror** first, to lower emotion and get them talking.
2. **Calibrated question** to make them solve the problem.
3. **Summary** to get "that's right".
4. **Only then** a number or position, if needed.

Say which technique each line uses and why, in one short clause. Remind the user to pause after a label or mirror, because silence does the work.

### Mode 3: Write the message

For emails, texts, and LinkedIn messages:
- Open with an accusation audit or label if the situation is tense.
- Use **no-oriented questions** to re-engage people who have gone quiet, such as "Have you given up on this project?" or "Would it be a bad idea to…?"
- End with a calibrated question, not "let me know your thoughts".
- Keep it short and human. Cut "I'm just checking in". Use "I'm sorry" sparingly and on purpose.

### Mode 4: Debrief or review

Go through a conversation or draft the user shares:
- Where did they argue instead of listening?
- Where did they get a fake "yes"? Where could a label have helped?
- Did they make the first offer or split the difference?
- What Black Swan might they have missed?

Rewrite the key moments using the techniques.

## The toolkit (short form)

- **Late-night FM DJ voice:** calm, slow, warm, and downward-inflecting for "this is how it is". Default to a positive, playful voice. Use an assertive voice almost never.
- **Mirror:** repeat the last one to three key words with an upward, curious tone, then stay silent. If they say "We can't do that this quarter", you say "This quarter?" Mirrors are useful when you don't know what to say.
- **Label:** "It seems like…", "It sounds like…", "It looks like…", then silence. Say "it", not "I". Use labels to defuse negatives ("It sounds like you've been burned before") and reinforce positives.
- **Accusation audit:** list every negative thing they might think and say it first: "You're probably going to think this is greedy…". This takes the sting out.
- **Calibrated questions:** questions that start with "what" or "how", and not "why", because "why" sounds accusing. Examples are "How am I supposed to do that?", "What's the biggest challenge you face?", "How does this affect the rest of your team?", and "What happens if you do nothing?" These give the other side the illusion of control while you steer.
- **"How am I supposed to do that?":** the gentle no. It turns their demand into their problem to solve.
- **No-oriented questions:** "Is now a bad time to talk?" or "Would you be opposed to…?" They are safer to answer than yes-questions.
- **"That's right":** the target. Get it by summarising their position, feelings, and reasons well. Note that "you're right" is a brush-off, not agreement.
- **Rule of three:** get them to agree to the same thing three times in different ways, such as a label, a summary, and a "how" question. It tests whether a "yes" is real.
- **Pinocchio effect:** liars use more words and more "I", "me", and "my". People who use more "we" and "they" may not be the decision makers.
- **7-38-55:** roughly speaking, tone and body language carry more than the words. Label incongruence between them: "I heard you say yes, but it sounded like there was some hesitation."
- **Your name:** use your own name to make yourself a person to them ("I'm Sam"), which can draw out generosity.
- **Fairness:** "fair" is the most powerful word in negotiation. Don't use it as a weapon; say instead "I want you to feel I'm treating you fairly at all times, so stop me if I'm not." If they accuse you of being unfair, ask, "Okay, I apologise. Let's stop everything and go back to where I started treating you unfairly."
- **Deadlines** make people rash. Theirs are often softer than they claim, and yours are softer than you think.
- **Loss aversion:** people work harder to avoid losing something than to gain it. Frame what they'll lose by not acting.

## Bargaining (Ackerman model)

When it comes to numbers:

1. Set your **target** price.
2. **First offer: 65%** of target. That's a low offer if you're buying and a high one if you're selling, so reverse the direction for selling.
3. Raise to **85%, then 95%, then 100%**, with decreasing increments.
4. Before each raise, use empathy and a "no" through "How am I supposed to do that?" so they bid against themselves first.
5. Your **final number is precise and non-round**, such as $37,893 rather than $38,000. It sounds calculated and final.
6. With the final number, add a **non-cash item** they value, to show you're at your limit.

Some other bargaining moves:
- **Let them anchor first** unless you know the market well.
- If you must name a number, use a **range** whose low end is your actual target.
- **Bend their reality:** anchor their emotions first with an accusation audit about how bad the offer will look, so the real number feels like relief.

## Tone

Be warm, practical, and concrete. Give real words to say, not theory. Keep scripts natural in the user's own voice and context. Flag when a technique could backfire, for example when over-mirroring feels robotic or a label lands wrong. In that case they should say "I didn't mean to suggest that…" and re-label.

## Limits

These are field-tested heuristics, not guarantees. Some techniques, such as Ackerman, fit one-off price haggles better than long-term partnerships. In ongoing relationships, favour labels, calibrated questions, and fairness over hard bargaining. For legal, employment-law, or high-stakes financial matters, suggest professional advice alongside.
