## 1. About Voss
- He is a former FBI lead international kidnapping negotiator.
- He founded the Black Swan Group, a negotiation training and consulting firm, and has taught at USC and Georgetown.
- His core argument is that classic "rational" negotiation theory, such as *Getting to Yes* with its BATNA and win-win models, underestimates emotion. Hostage negotiation shows that people are irrational and emotional, so you start there.

## 2. The book chapter by chapter
1. **The new rules:** Effective negotiation is information gathering and behaviour influence. People are emotional, and Kahneman's System 1 thinking drives decisions.
2. **Be a mirror:** Slow down, use the late-night FM DJ voice, and mirror. Focus on the other side, not on your own argument.
3. **Don't feel their pain, label it:** Tactical empathy. Labels, the accusation audit, and defusing negatives early.
4. **Beware "yes", master "no":** "No" protects autonomy and starts real talk. No-oriented questions. Using silence after an email that has gone quiet.
5. **Trigger the two words that transform any negotiation:** "That's right", reached through summaries. "You're right" is a brush-off.
6. **Bend their reality:** Deadlines, fairness, anchoring emotions, loss aversion, letting them go first, ranges, non-monetary terms, and odd numbers.
7. **Create the illusion of control:** Calibrated questions. "How am I supposed to do that?" Self-control: if you can't control yourself, you can't influence others.
8. **Guarantee execution:** "Yes" is nothing without "how". Find the people behind the table. Rule of three, Pinocchio effect, 7-38-55, and using your name.
9. **Bargain hard:** Negotiator types, taking a punch, strategic umbrage, anchoring, and the Ackerman model.
10. **Find the Black Swan:** Unknown unknowns. Leverage (positive, negative, and normative), the other side's religion or worldview, and the "similarity principle".

## 3. Techniques in depth

### Mirroring
- Repeat the last one to three words, or the most important ones, and pause for at least four seconds.
- **Formula for confrontation without conflict:** use the FM DJ voice, start with "I'm sorry…", mirror, stay silent, label, and repeat.
- **Example:** If the boss says "We need two copies of all the paperwork", you say "I'm sorry, two copies?" They explain, and you learn the real reason.

### Labeling
- Use "It seems / sounds / looks like…". Never start with "I'm hearing", because it makes the label about you.
- Pause after the label and let it land.
- **Negative first:** label fears to defuse them before addressing anything else.
- **Examples:**
  - "It seems like you're worried this will take more of your time than it's worth."
  - "It sounds like the last vendor let you down."
  - "It looks like this matters a lot to you."

### Accusation audit
- List every negative thing the other side might think or feel about you or your request, and say them first, in strong terms.
- **Example:** "You're probably going to think I'm being unreasonable. It might even seem like I don't appreciate what you've done for me. And you might feel this is the worst possible timing."
- The other side then often reassures you.

### "No"
- "No" means "not yet", "I'm not comfortable", or "I need more information". It doesn't end the conversation.
- **No-oriented questions:**
  - "Is now a bad time to talk?"
  - "Would it be ridiculous to…?"
  - "Are you against…?"
  - "Do you want this project to fail?"
- **For an email that's gone quiet,** send one line: "Have you given up on this project?"
- **Three kinds of yes:**
  - **Counterfeit:** said to escape.
  - **Confirmation:** a reflexive yes.
  - **Commitment:** the real one, which usually comes with action.

### "That's right"
- Build a summary by paraphrasing their position and labelling their emotions. When it's accurate, they say "that's right".
- That moment is when their resistance drops and they feel understood.

### Calibrated questions
- Start with "what" or "how", and occasionally "which". Avoid "why", "can", "is", and "do", because they invite yes or no answers or sound accusing.
- **Useful bank:**
  - What about this is important to you?
  - How can I help make this better for us?
  - How would you like me to proceed?
  - What is it that brought us into this situation?
  - How can we solve this problem?
  - What's the objective? / What are we trying to accomplish here?
  - How am I supposed to do that?
  - How does this fit into what the objective is?
  - What does the rest of your team think? How does this affect them?
  - What are we up against here? What's the biggest challenge you face?
  - What happens if you do nothing?
  - How will we know we're on track?
  - What does success look like?
- **Use them to:**
  - expose the people behind the table, by asking how the deal affects everyone else and how on board the people not on the call are;
  - test whether a deal will actually be carried out;
  - say no gently.

### Saying no without saying no
- Start with "How am I supposed to do that?"
- Then try "Your offer is very generous, I'm sorry, that just doesn't work for me."
- Then "I'm sorry, but I'm afraid I just can't do that."
- Then "I'm sorry, no."
- A softer final no is just "No."

### Bending reality
- **Fairness:** watch for "we just want what's fair". Respond with "It seems like you feel I've treated you unfairly" or "What's fair?"
- **Anchor emotions:** use an accusation audit before the bad news.
- **Let them go first:** they may anchor better than you expected.
- **Ranges:** an anchor like "Places like X pay $130–170k" pulls the other side up.
- **Non-monetary terms:** add things that are cheap to you and valuable to them.
- **Odd numbers:** precise figures seem researched.
- **Surprise gifts:** an unexpected concession triggers reciprocity.
- **Loss framing:** "What do you stand to lose if this doesn't happen by Friday?"

### Salary playbook (from the book)
1. Be pleasantly persistent on non-salary terms.
2. Salary terms without success terms are a risk, so define what success looks like in the role.
3. Ask "What does it take to be successful here?" When they answer, they effectively become your mentor.
4. Spark their interest in your success and get a feeling of "unconditional positive regard".

## 4. Negotiator types
- **Analyst:**
  - Prepared, methodical, and dislikes surprises.
  - Their silence means they're thinking.
  - They reciprocate slowly and may see concessions as new information.
  - Use clear data, don't push, and say "no" early so they can say no back.
- **Accommodator:**
  - Values time spent building the relationship and is friendly and chatty.
  - May agree without the ability to deliver.
  - Use calibrated questions about implementation.
- **Assertive:**
  - Believes time is money and wants to be heard.
  - Can't listen until they feel heard.
  - Use mirrors, labels and summaries, and get to "that's right".

Most people assume the other side is the same type they are, which Voss calls "the golden rule trap". Treat others the way *they* need to be treated.

## 5. Ackerman: a worked example (buying)
- Your target is $20,000.
- **Offer 1 (65%):** $13,000. Expect a "no". Use empathy and calibrated questions, and get them to counter.
- **Offer 2 (85%):** $17,000.
- **Offer 3 (95%):** $19,000.
- **Offer 4 (100%):** $19,870, a precise number, plus a non-cash item such as flexible collection timing.
- Between steps, use "How am I supposed to do that?" or "I'm sorry, that doesn't work for me" so they bid against themselves.
- **For selling,** reverse the direction: start at about 135% of target and come down in shrinking steps to a precise final number.

## 6. Black Swans
- Black Swans are unknown unknowns: information that, once found, changes the deal. Examples are a hidden deadline, a personal motive, or a constraint they assume you already know about.
- **How to find them:**
  - Use face time over email.
  - Listen for the unspoken.
  - Watch for anomalies and odd demands, which are clues rather than craziness.
  - Find their "religion": the worldview that makes their position make sense.
- **Three types of leverage:**
  - **Positive:** you can give them what they want.
  - **Negative:** you can hurt them. Use this carefully, through labels, not threats.
  - **Normative:** use their own stated norms and standards.
- **Similarity principle:** people trust people like them, so find genuine commonality.
- **"Crazy" usually means** they are ill-informed, constrained, or have interests you don't know about.

## 7. One-sheet template
```
GOAL (best case, justified, written down):
SUMMARY (facts they'd say "that's right" to):
LABELS / ACCUSATION AUDIT (3–5):
CALIBRATED QUESTIONS (3–5; include "behind the table" questions):
NON-CASH ITEMS TO OFFER:
ACKERMAN PLAN (target, 65/85/95/100, precise final, non-cash closer):
COUNTERPART TYPE & HOW TO ADAPT:
POSSIBLE BLACK SWANS TO PROBE:
```

## 8. Common mistakes
- Arguing and explaining instead of listening.
- Treating "yes" as agreement.
- Using "why".
- Splitting the difference to end the discomfort.
- Setting a bottom line instead of an ambitious goal, then anchoring on it.
- Rushing.
- Ignoring the people behind the table.
- Using an assertive voice.
- Saying "you're right" or getting "you're right" back and thinking you've won.
- Over-using techniques until they sound scripted.

## 9. Scenario playbooks (quick)
- **Raise:**
  - Start with an accusation audit ("This might feel like it's coming out of nowhere…").
  - Summarise your contributions to reach "that's right".
  - Ask a calibrated question ("What would it take for this to happen this cycle?").
  - Give a range anchor and add non-salary terms.
- **Client pushes back on price:**
  - Label ("It seems like the budget is tighter than you expected").
  - Ask a calibrated question ("What are you comparing this to?").
  - Remove scope, never just discount.
  - Ask "How am I supposed to do that?" if they push for a cut that leaves scope unchanged.
- **Prospect goes quiet:**
  - Send a one-line no-oriented email: "Have you given up on [project]?"
- **Angry customer:**
  - Use the FM DJ voice and an accusation audit ("You probably feel we've completely let you down").
  - Use labels, then summarise to reach "that's right".
  - Then ask "How can we make this right?"
- **Vendor or landlord:**
  - Ask them to name the number first.
  - Use Ackerman, a precise final figure, and a non-cash sweetener such as a longer term or fast payment.
