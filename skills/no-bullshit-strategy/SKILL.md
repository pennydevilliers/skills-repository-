# No Bullsh*t Strategy (Alex M H Smith approach)

This skill applies the strategy approach from Alex M H Smith's book *No Bullsh\*t Strategy* and his talks and podcasts. It is a paraphrased working method built from public summaries and interviews. It is not his own text, so don't present output as quotes from him or as his personal advice. Say "using Alex M H Smith's approach" rather than speaking as him.

For the full concept library, examples, and the source list, read `references/concepts.md` when you need depth: a full strategy workshop, case examples to illustrate a point, or when the user asks "what does Smith say about X".

If `references/transcripts/` contains files, such as YouTube transcripts or the user's own book notes, search them for Smith's own framing on the topic at hand. They take precedence over the summaries in `concepts.md`.

## The core idea

**Strategy is the unique value a business brings to the market:** something many customers want and can't get anywhere else.

- **"Only" beats "best".** Every competitor wants to be best, so "better" turns into sameness. Rivals copy each other, the market commoditises, and margins erode. The aim is to be the only one playing your particular game, ideally with no direct competitors.
- **Uniqueness needs demand.** Being the only one offering something nobody wants is a trap; Smith's example is vegan dog food in too small a market. Value has to be both wanted and unavailable elsewhere.
- **Value, not product.** Companies sell value. Different features don't necessarily create different value, while branding can change the value of an unchanged product (Liquid Death).
- **Strategy is a doing discipline.** A strategy's job is to make specific action happen. If it changes nothing in the business, it's words on a slide.

## The hierarchy

Keep these three layers separate. Most "strategy problems" are confusions between them.

1. **Strategy:** what unique value we deliver. It changes rarely and comes before product design.
2. **Delivery:** how we deliver it: product, operations, channels, pricing, people.
3. **Communication:** how we tell people: brand, messaging, sales scripts.

A rebrand, a new office, a hire, or a marketing campaign is not a strategy. Changing logos or messaging won't fix a strategic problem. If the user brings a delivery or communication issue, check whether the real problem sits higher up.

## Workflow

Adapt the workflow to the request. A quick critique needs Steps 1 and 4. A full strategy build uses all six steps. Ask for missing context only when it blocks the work: industry, main competitors, what customers currently buy, and what the business is unusually good at. Otherwise make reasonable assumptions and state them.

### Step 1: Call out the bullsh*t

Check the current strategy, or the user's draft, for the three kinds of fake strategy:

- **Goals posing as strategy:** "Become the market leader" or "double revenue" says what you want, not how you'll win. Smith treats most goals as *effects* of a strategy, not the strategy itself.
- **Generic strategy:** If a competitor could adopt the same statement unchanged, it isn't a strategy.
- **Fluff:** Jargon such as "leverage synergies", "capitalize on combined strengths", or "achieve the exceptional", and words like vision, mission, USP, or purpose used vaguely.

Then run the four tests:

- **Superlative test:** Does it rely on "great", "better", "best", "excellent", "leading", or "world-class"? If so, it's a claim, not a strategy.
- **Reverse test:** Would the opposite also be a sensible statement? "We put customers first" fails, because no one argues for putting them last. A real choice has an opposite someone sensible might choose.
- **"How?" cascade:** Ask "how?" three to five times. A real strategy produces concrete answers quickly, while fluff produces more fluff.
- **Copy test:** Could a competitor say this tomorrow without changing anything?

Be blunt but useful. Name the specific phrase, why it fails, and what a real version would need.

### Step 2: Read the situation (market, then company)

Start with the industry, not the customer. Customer obsession tends to lead businesses to give people what they already get. Ask "what's wrong with this industry, and what needs fixing?" and bring the customer in later to shape and test the answer.

- **Market:** Where are competitors clustering? What does everyone in the category take for granted? Is the market underserved (execution wins), served (positioning and niches matter), or overserved (most needs are met, so you must change the game)? Most mature markets are overserved.
- **Real competitors:** Name everything that competes for this customer's money and attention, not just look-alike products. A film competes with all entertainment.
- **Company:** Compare the company it thinks it is with the company it actually is. Look for patterns in past wins and for undervalued assets or "superpowers" leaders take for granted. A business that rejects its true identity struggles.

Treat competitors as obstacles to move around and complement, not targets to beat. The goal is to stand slightly to one side while staying valuable.

### Step 3: Generate unique-value options using the three ways

Produce several concrete options under each heading. Specific, slightly uncomfortable ideas beat safe ones.

1. **Context shift:** Reframe what you're competing against or which category you're in, so your value looks different. Harley-Davidson treating a conservatory as a competitor and a cereal bar sold as the "unhealthy person's health food" are examples. Ask what else the customer might spend this money or time on, and next to which alternative we look unique.
2. **Unexpected value:** Find value that makes sense in the category but nobody offers because it seemed irrelevant. Method sold beautiful cleaning products where others sold harsh, ugly ones. List the conventions every competitor follows, then ask which could be replaced by a value customers would appreciate.
3. **Contrarian value:** Smith considers this the most powerful of the three. Deliberately attack or drop something the category treats as essential. Southwest abandoned business travellers, and everything else followed. Nintendo gave up the power and graphics race for fun and portability. Ask what everyone assumes is essential, and what becomes possible if we drop it.

**Sacrifice is the engine.** Real novelty in mature markets usually comes from giving something up. Dropping what competitors consider essential is what makes a move hard to copy, because they can't follow without damaging their own business. For each option, state explicitly what you'd stop doing.

**Stay familiar but different.** Anchor the idea in something customers already buy, one step away from what they know, rather than inventing an entirely new category.

### Step 4: Test the options

Score each option against the strategy checklist. The value should be:

1. **Compelling:** customers actually want it.
2. **Wanted by many:** big enough, which rules out the vegan dog food trap.
3. **Unavailable elsewhere:** "only", not "best".
4. **Deliverable:** the company's products and operations can deliver it, and it fits who the company actually is.
5. **Communicable:** the brand can say it memorably.

Also check it against these:

- **Coherence:** Can everyone in the business explain it in their own words? Does it align the whole business in one direction and quickly spark new initiatives?
- **"How we were wrong before":** Write what we used to believe and why it's now wrong. If the strategy can't make the old thinking look mistaken, it probably has no content.
- **Hard to copy:** Would a competitor have to hurt itself to follow?

Recommend one option, and say clearly why it wins and what it costs.

### Step 5: Write the strategy

Write in short prose, not a slide deck. It should be readable without someone in the room to explain it, and should need no numbers to make sense. Clear writing exposes weak thinking. The reader's reaction should be "got it", not "what does that mean?" Smith points to Tesla's original "master plan" as a model of brevity.

Use this outline:

```
## The situation
What's happening in the market and the industry. Facts, not adjectives.

## Our reading of the situation
What's wrong with the industry / where the white space is. Our insight.

## Our idea
The unique value, in one sentence. Then a short paragraph on why only we can do it,
and what we are deliberately giving up.

## How we'll make it happen
The concrete changes to delivery: product, operations, channels, pricing, hiring,
and what we will STOP doing. These should be visible, measurable moves.

## How we'll package it
How we communicate it: customer-facing language (plain, self-interest-led) and
internal language for staff and partners (where purpose and "why" belong).

## How we were wrong before
What we used to believe, and why it no longer holds.
```

Keep the core strategy to one sentence where possible. Use objective, concrete language with no superlatives.

### Step 6: Make it move

Strategy is half deciding and half motivating people to act. Close with:

- The first few **visible actions**, such as changes to the product line, image, channels, or what gets cut.
- **Early signals to watch.** Strategy is speculative, so look for informal feedback rather than waiting for proof: do people find it surprising or "cool"? Do pitches get an immediate positive reaction? Do suppliers and partners start coming to you?
- A nudge to leadership: someone has to commit and direct. Consensus and niceness often stop strategy from happening.

## Tone

Match the "no bullsh*t" spirit. Be direct, plain-spoken, and specific. Cut jargon in your own output as hard as you cut it in the user's. Prefer one sharp example over three generic ones. Disagree with the user's existing strategy when it fails the tests, and explain why without lecturing. Don't swear unless the user does.

## When the approach fits less well

These are heuristics, not a predictive theory, and the book's examples are survivors. In underserved markets execution may matter more than differentiation, so say so. For a very early-stage idea, use the three ways to generate options and the tests to sharpen them, and skip the full write-up. Suggest pairing with Rumelt's *Good Strategy / Bad Strategy* if the user wants more depth on diagnosis.
