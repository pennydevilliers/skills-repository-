## 1. About Smith
- He is the author of *No Bullsh\*t Strategy: A Founder's Guide to Gaining Competitive Advantage with a Strategy That Actually Works*, a short book whose audiobook runs about three hours.
- He founded the consultancy Basic Arts, which focuses on brands that are a "category of one", meaning they have no direct competition.
- He has advised brands including Porsche, P&G, and Roche, and writes a Substack newsletter, *The Rare Mind*.
- He appears widely on podcasts: Ditching Hourly, Renegade Marketing, Strategy Meets Reality, Lancefield on the Line, Curious Leadership (Monkhouse), WARC Talks, and Leaders in Strategy.

## 2. Definitions
- **Business:** a system people build to put value into the world and get value back, usually as money. To take in more value, you have to put out more.
- **Strategy:** the unique value a business brings to the market.
- **Unique value:** value customers want and can't get anywhere else.
- **White space:** the gap between what customers need and what's currently available. Great strategies exploit it.
- **Delivery:** how the value reaches people: product, operations, channels, and other physical parts of the business.
- **Communication / branding:** how the value is expressed: messaging, brand, and sales scripts. Marketers usually control only this layer, which is why marketing-led "strategy" often fails.
- **Positioning vs. strategy:** positioning usually happens after the product exists. Strategy comes before product design and organises the whole business.
- **Macro vs. micro:** strategy is the macro move. Tactics such as opening an office, tweaking a product, or hiring are not strategy, even when presented as such. Smith criticises the chess analogy because it implies a strategy is a sequence of small moves.
- **"Drawing a circle":** strategy draws a boundary around the business and sets one blueprint for product, distribution, and marketing.
- **Multiple levels:** the same logic applies to a business, a product line, or an individual's career.

## 3. Why "only" beats "best"
- "Better" equals the same: doing what everyone does, slightly more.
- Head-to-head competition clusters companies together, forcing cost cuts and higher spending for any edge, so margins erode.
- The market rarely rewards "best". The winners of a "best" contest tend to be whoever has the most awareness, availability, and budget.
- Being the "only" lets a company escape competitive costs such as marketing arms races, price cuts, and constant reinvestment.
- In Smith's framing, great companies have no competitors.
- **Segmentation example:** Häagen-Dazs and Ben & Jerry's share a category without fighting head-on, because each owns a different niche.
- **Category lives in the customer's head:** Apple built a walled garden inside the phone category.

## 4. The three kinds of bullsh*t strategy
1. **Goals as strategy:** "Double sales", "become #1". These are aspirations or effects, not a plan for winning.
2. **Generic strategy:** anything a competitor could adopt unchanged.
3. **Fluff:** buzzwords like "leverage synergies", "capitalize on combined strengths", "motivate the remarkable", and "achieve the exceptional".

Tests (described in SKILL.md): superlative test, reverse test, "how?" cascade, copy test, "how we were wrong before", and coherence.

## 5. The three ways to unique value
### Context shift
- Frame your value against a different category or competitor set.
- Harley-Davidson treats a conservatory or other big lifestyle purchases, not other motorbikes, as its real competitor.
- A struggling cereal bar became the "unhealthy person's health food". It was healthier than chocolate bars, so it became the obvious choice in the candy aisle.
- Real competitors are anything competing for the customer's attention and money. A film competes with all entertainment.

### Unexpected value
- Look past category conventions for value that makes sense but that nobody offers because it seemed irrelevant.
- Method sold cleaning products in beautiful packaging people didn't hide under the sink, which signalled gentleness and quality, while competitors sold power.
- Disney's rides are ordinary. Families pay for proximity to beloved characters and stories that no other park can offer.
- **Exercise:** list every convention in the category, then ask which could be replaced with something customers would appreciate.

### Contrarian value
- Smith considers this the most powerful route. Escape the cluster by rejecting something the category treats as fundamental.
- Southwest Airlines walked away from business travellers. It dropped business and first class, flew from less convenient airports, used a standardised fleet, turned planes around faster, and included two free checked bags. The sacrifice drove every operational choice. JetBlue copied the model and added comfort. When Southwest later added assigned seating and premium options, it diluted its focus.
- Nintendo gave up the power and graphics race against Xbox and PlayStation, and won on fun, form, and portability.
- Domino's also stripped back parts of its industry's standard model.
- **Sacrifice drives innovation:** in mature markets the low-hanging fruit is gone, and dropping an "essential" opens possibilities that were impossible before. Competitors can't follow without hurting their own business. This echoes Drucker's view that strategy means saying no.
- **Practical start:** look at competitors and stop doing the things you're not especially good at anyway.

**Exercise:** spend about 15 minutes on each of the three ways for your own business.

## 6. Market stages
- **Underserved:** demand exceeds supply. Execution wins, so deliver what people already want, fast and efficiently.
- **Served:** good options exist. Competition is about positioning and niches.
- **Overserved:** most needs are met, so you have to change the market and create demand for things people didn't know they wanted. Smith argues that most markets today are overserved.

## 7. Reading the company
- Compare the company you think you are with the company you actually are.
- Read signals from both the market (what people want) and inside the company (where it has leverage).
- Look for patterns across past wins. Leaders often undervalue their real strengths because of preconceived ideas.
- Hidden strategic assets often sit inside current operations.
- Double down on existing superpowers.
- A "self-loathing" business that rejects its true identity is likely to fail.
- Good strategy is a market–capability fit: what the market needs, matched with what you're unusually good at.

## 8. Language and jargon
- Vision, mission, USP, purpose, and proposition have blurry meanings, so use plain language instead.
- Smith reads a **mission** as a clear order, not a purpose statement.
- Most **goals** are effects of strategy, not things you pursue directly.
- A better prompt than "what's our vision?" is "what's the new thing we want to create that didn't exist before?"
- Avoid subjective superlatives such as "excellent", "superior", and "top-notch", and describe things objectively.
- **Purpose / "why":** valuable for staff and partners, but customers buy on self-interest. Patagonia runs a commercial strategy for customers and a purpose message for partners.

## 9. Writing and communicating strategy
- Write prose, not slides. It must be interpretable without a presenter.
- Keep the core strategy to one sentence. A strong strategy is short and needs no numbers. Tesla's original master plan is the model.
- Clear writing exposes weak thinking. The target reaction is "got it", not "what does that mean?" or "how?"
- **Outline:** situation, then our reading of it, then our idea, then how we'll make it happen, then how we'll package it. Add a "how we were wrong before" section.
- Translate internal strategy language into customer-facing language.
- **Pitch testing:** rewrite a pitch deck around the strategy and watch for immediate, clear positive reactions. This is easier in B2B.

## 10. Marketing ideas from his talks
- Once a tactic works, everyone copies it and its effect fades, and cheap tech plus sceptical customers speed this up.
- Make marketing that's hard to copy or produce: original research, live events, deep relationships, distinctive expertise, and genuine scarcity.
- When easy things become abundant, friction becomes an advantage.
- For service businesses and solopreneurs, a distinctive point of view, shared through writing, speaking, and LinkedIn, is itself unique value.
- Marketers commonly get three things wrong: they over-focus on communications and ignore the rest of the business, overcomplicate strategy, and lean on jargon.
- Many rebrands solve the wrong problem, and the same misdiagnosis happens in sales.

## 11. Leadership and execution
- Strategy is a doing discipline, not a thinking one. A bold, distinct action you learn from beats a perfect idea you never act on.
- Strategy is half deciding what to do and half motivating people to do it.
- People want to be told what's wanted so they can act. Leaders must give clear direction and constraints.
- Consensus-seeking and niceness can kill strategy. Directing may make you unpopular.
- Strategy operates at the edge of the unknown, so you can't fully validate it with data. Commit, then watch early informal signals: is it surprising, is it "cool", do suppliers start coming to you?
- Zoom out and sense the whole landscape rather than over-analysing detail. Most people find industry-level thinking uncomfortable.
- **Diagnose industry problems before customer problems.** Tesla saw that EVs were cheap-feeling cars that were expensive to make, so it started with a high-end sports car. Customer obsession can kill good strategy, because customers ask for what they already get. Bring the customer in at the end, as in Rick Rubin's view that the audience comes last.
- Strategy changes rarely, while brand and execution layers change faster. A bold strategic period can build brand equity that pays out for decades, until overexposure brings diminishing returns.
- You have to change something real in the business, or results won't change.
